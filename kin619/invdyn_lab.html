SES 619 — Link-Segment Mechanics

Inverse Dynamics Lab

Combine ground reaction forces with joint-center coordinates to solve 2D sagittal-plane joint reaction forces and net muscle moments, bottom-up from the ground contact point through as many segments as you define — with a live free-body-diagram schematic showing the equations plugged in at whatever instant you're looking at. Runs entirely in your browser — nothing is uploaded anywhere.

1. Subject & conventions

Used to look up de Leva's (1996) segment mass %, center-of-mass %, and radius-of-gyration % for whichever segment type you assign each link below.

Required — converts de Leva's mass percentages into kg for the Newton-Euler equations.

Everything is converted to meters internally. Getting this wrong is the single easiest way to end up with wrong-looking forces and moments — there's no safe default, so pick deliberately. As a gut check: your Ankle→Knee distance should compute to somewhere around 0.4 m (see the "Segment lengths" sanity-check table after you compute).

In the same units and vertical coordinate system as your joint markers (usually 0).

Coordinate convention: this tool only needs two roles — horizontal (anteroposterior) and vertical (positive = up) — it never assumes those are literally called "X" and "Y" in your data. Whatever your columns are actually called, you always assign each one's role explicitly in the dropdowns below, and that's final regardless of anything on this page — nothing here can silently overrule your own choice. If your data also has a mediolateral column, it isn't used here — this tool is sagittal-plane (2D) only, so a third axis has no home in the current calculation. Force and marker data must share the same horizontal origin — if your force plate and motion-capture system don't already share one, translate one dataset before pasting. Force data must be in newtons (N). g = 9.81 m/s².

This page is shared and anyone can use it — different labs letter their axes differently, so there's no built-in default. These three fields only drive the auto-suggested column in each dropdown below (e.g. type Y and Z here and a column named "RANK_Y" gets suggested for the horizontal role, "RANK_Z" for vertical; set Side to Right and a column named "LANK_Y" gets skipped in favor of "RANK_Y") — set them before parsing, since that's when the first guess happens. Leave axisHoriz/axisVert blank and every dropdown just starts on "None," nothing gets auto-picked. Either way, every suggestion is only a starting point: check it, and change it if it's wrong.

2. Segment chain

List the points of your chain in order from the ground contact end upward — e.g. Toe, Ankle, Knee, Hip, Shoulder, Elbow, Wrist. Each consecutive pair becomes one rigid segment; the tool solves for the joint reaction force and net muscle moment at every point after the first, working up from the ground.

Segment parameters

Each segment's mass %, center-of-mass % (measured from its proximal — upper — end), and radius of gyration % come from de Leva (1996), auto-filled once you pick a segment type below (override the sex above and these update). Segment length is computed directly from your pasted marker distance, not entered by hand. If a segment doesn't match a standard de Leva type, choose Custom and enter the three parameters yourself.

3. Your data

Paste force/COP data and marker data together in one table, or keep them separate — whichever matches how your data already sits in your spreadsheet. Either way, nothing is assumed from column order or position; you assign every column's role explicitly below.

Ground reaction force data

Values will be multiplied by body mass × 9.81 (from step 1) to convert to newtons — e.g. a peak vertical value around 1.0–1.3 is typical for walking when it means "times body weight," not 1.0–1.3 N. This is a common export convention (Visual3D "normalized" processed outputs, among others).

Each "pass" here is a 1st-order zero-phase filter, not the 2nd-order-per-pass design most biomechanics software uses — the number you type above is automatically pre-corrected so the filter's real, delivered −3dB cutoff matches what you entered, for any number of passes.

During swing, vertical force is near zero and the reported COP is noise — not a real contact point. Frames where |Fy| falls below this threshold have their force zeroed (which also zeroes their contribution to every downstream moment, COP noise included). Set to 0 to disable.

No free-moment (Mz) input is needed here: by definition, the center of pressure is the point where the in-plane (sagittal-bending) moment of the ground's force distribution is zero — that's what makes it the COP. Any Mz your plate reports is about the vertical axis (friction/torsional), a different axis than this 2D sagittal model.

Joint-center coordinate data

Using the same time column and sampling rate as the force data above — this came from one combined paste (step 3), so there's only one time base to set.

If this is sampled at a different rate than your force data (common — mocap is often slower), velocity and acceleration are computed at THIS native marker rate first, and only the resulting acceleration curves are interpolated onto the force data's time base — not the raw positions. Differentiating after interpolating onto a finer time base was tried first and produced garbage (near-zero accelerations with spikes at the original sample times), since a linearly-interpolated curve is piecewise straight and its second derivative is zero except at the joints between pieces. The analysis window is trimmed to where the two datasets overlap.

Kinematic data usually needs a much lower cutoff than force data — differentiating twice to get acceleration amplifies high-frequency noise enormously. If you're unsure, check Filter Compare first. As with the force data above, the cutoff you type here is pre-corrected so the filter's real, delivered −3dB point matches it exactly, regardless of pass count.

4. Outputs

Choose which time series appear in each joint's section of the results. You can change any of these after computing too — the results update instantly, no need to recompute.

Joint angle is the relative angle between the two segments meeting at a joint (distal segment's angle − proximal segment's angle; 0° = segments in line, so a straight knee reads 0°; for the ankle, where the foot sits at roughly a right angle to the shank in neutral, that 90° is removed so neutral also reads ≈ 0°). The ankle's 0° is exactly "foot line perpendicular to shank": with the "true proximal landmark" (heel) option on, the foot line is heel→toe, close to the sole; without it, the foot line is toe→ankle, which tilts up from the sole by several degrees, so a neutral ankle will read off zero by that tilt. Angles and angular velocities are reported in degrees; power is still computed with ω in rad/s, as P = M·ω requires. Joint angular velocity is the relative angular velocity of the two segments meeting at a joint (distal segment's ω − proximal segment's ω), plotted in the same sign convention as the moment — so joint power P = M·ω is simply the product of the two curves above it, and is positive (energy generated by the muscles) whenever M and ω share a sign, negative (energy absorbed) when they don't. The top joint of your chain has no segment above it, so it gets moment and reaction force but no ω, power or work.

Work is the time integral of joint power (trapezoidal rule), taken over the phase you choose here. Net work sums generation and absorption; positive and negative work integrate each part separately (split exactly at every zero crossing), which is why net = positive + negative. "Use current frame" buttons work after computing — scrub the results' play bar to the instant you want, then press one. The chosen phase is shaded orange on every chart. This sets the starting phase for every joint (and resets them all when changed); each joint's own section then has its own phase picker, so e.g. the ankle can use push-off while the knee uses early stance.

How to read the free-body diagrams

Each segment is drawn isolated from its neighbors — the classic free-body-diagram convention — animated from your actual joint-marker positions at the selected instant. Green = weight (mg) at the COM, navy = the known force at the segment's distal end (the ground reaction force at COP for the ground-contact segment, or the reaction handed down from the segment below for every other segment), teal = the solved joint reaction force at the proximal end, red arcs = real net muscle moments, each centered on the point it acts about, dashed purple arc = the segment's own I·α (its rotational equation of motion, plotted at the COM — not an applied force, but what the moment equation is actually balancing against). Reaction force / moment are what the proximal segment applies onto the distal segment at that joint by this tool's own solve direction (Newton's third law gives you the reverse — use "Flip moment sign" above for moments in Winter's convention). The GRF only enters the model once, at the ground-contact segment — every joint above it inherits the GRF's effect indirectly, since each segment's own solved reaction force/moment becomes the next segment's known input, by Newton's third law. The equation panels name that first block's terms GRF_x/GRF_y/M_GRF explicitly instead of the generic R_dist/M_dist used everywhere else. Equation-panel values always show this tool's own raw solve direction, unaffected by the sign-flip/normalize display options above.

Joint work

Peak values by joint

Solved bottom-up per segment using Newton's second law (ΣF = m·acom) and the rotational equivalent about each segment's own center of mass (ΣMcom = Icom·α), starting from the ground-contact segment (known force = the filtered GRF at COP; known moment = zero, since COP is defined as the point where the ground's in-plane moment is zero) and carrying each solved joint reaction force/moment, negated per Newton's third law, up as the "known" distal input for the next segment. Segment center of mass and radius of gyration use de Leva's (1996) percentages for whichever type you assign each link; segment length comes from the actual distance between your two pasted joint markers (median over the trial) rather than a separately-entered length. de Leva's tables define each segment's own proximal anthropometric landmark precisely (e.g. the heel, not the ankle, for the foot) — for the shank/thigh/upper-arm/forearm that already coincides with the real joint (ankle, knee, shoulder, elbow), but for the ground-contact segment it usually doesn't, since what connects to the next segment up the chain is a joint (the ankle), not the segment's true anthropometric end. Step 2's "true proximal landmark is different" option handles this: when enabled, mass/COM/inertia are computed from that landmark (e.g. heel) to the distal point (e.g. toe), while the joint reaction force/moment at the actual joint (e.g. ankle) are still solved and reported there, unaffected — leave it off and the tool falls back to using the joint itself for both, same as before. Linear and angular accelerations are computed by second-order central finite difference on the filtered position/angle time series, and segment angular velocity by first-order central difference. Joint power is the net moment times the relative angular velocity of the two segments at that joint, P = M·(ωdistal − ωproximal), and joint work is its trapezoidal time integral over the chosen phase.
