SES 619 — Link-Segment Mechanics

Inverse Dynamics Lab

Combine ground reaction forces with joint-center coordinates to solve 2D sagittal-plane joint reaction forces and net muscle moments, bottom-up from the ground contact point through as many segments as you define — with a live free-body-diagram schematic showing the equations plugged in at whatever instant you're looking at. Runs entirely in your browser — nothing is uploaded anywhere.

1. Subject & conventions

Used to look up de Leva's (1996) segment mass %, center-of-mass %, and radius-of-gyration % for whichever segment type you assign each link below.

Required — converts de Leva's mass percentages into kg for the Newton-Euler equations.

Everything is converted to meters internally.

In the same units and vertical coordinate system as your joint markers (usually 0).

Coordinate convention: X = horizontal (anteroposterior), Y = vertical (positive = up), in both your force data and your marker data, sharing the same horizontal origin — if your force plate and motion-capture system don't already share one, translate one dataset before pasting. Force data must be in newtons (N). g = 9.81 m/s².

2. Segment chain

List the points of your chain in order from the ground contact end upward — e.g. Toe, Ankle, Knee, Hip, Shoulder, Elbow, Wrist. Each consecutive pair becomes one rigid segment; the tool solves for the joint reaction force and net muscle moment at every point after the first, working up from the ground.

Segment parameters

Each segment's mass %, center-of-mass % (measured from its proximal — upper — end), and radius of gyration % come from de Leva (1996), auto-filled once you pick a segment type below (override the sex above and these update). Segment length is computed directly from your pasted marker distance, not entered by hand. If a segment doesn't match a standard de Leva type, choose Custom and enter the three parameters yourself.

3. Ground reaction force data

No free-moment (Mz) input is needed here: by definition, the center of pressure is the point where the in-plane (sagittal-bending) moment of the ground's force distribution is zero — that's what makes it the COP. Any Mz your plate reports is about the vertical axis (friction/torsional), a different axis than this 2D sagittal model.

4. Joint-center coordinate data

If this is sampled at a different rate than your force data (common — mocap is often slower), it's linearly interpolated onto the force data's time base once both are parsed; the analysis window is trimmed to where the two overlap.

Kinematic data usually needs a much lower cutoff than force data — differentiating twice to get acceleration amplifies high-frequency noise enormously. If you're unsure, check Filter Compare first.

Joint kinetics vs. time

Reaction force / moment are what the proximal segment applies onto the distal segment at this joint (Newton's third law gives you the reverse). Click either chart to jump the schematic below to that instant.

Free-body-diagram schematic

Stick figure drawn from your actual joint-marker positions at the selected instant. Grey = weight (at each segment's COM), navy = the ground reaction force at COP, teal = solved joint reaction forces, amber arcs = solved net muscle moments (arrow direction = sign, counterclockwise-positive).

Peak values by joint

Solved bottom-up per segment using Newton's second law (ΣF = m·acom) and the rotational equivalent about each segment's own center of mass (ΣMcom = Icom·α), starting from the ground-contact segment (known force = the filtered GRF at COP; known moment = zero, since COP is defined as the point where the ground's in-plane moment is zero) and carrying each solved joint reaction force/moment, negated per Newton's third law, up as the "known" distal input for the next segment. Segment center of mass and radius of gyration use de Leva's (1996) percentages for whichever type you assign each link; segment length comes from the actual distance between your two pasted joint markers (median over the trial) rather than a separately-entered length. One simplification worth flagging: de Leva's tables define each segment's own proximal anthropometric landmark precisely (e.g. the heel, not the ankle, for the foot) — this tool instead uses whichever joint marker connects to the next segment up the chain as that reference point, which is exact for the shank/thigh/upper-arm/forearm (ankle, knee, shoulder, elbow all coincide with the real landmark) but an approximation for the ground-contact segment if your first chain point isn't already the true proximal landmark for its type. Linear and angular accelerations are computed by second-order central finite difference on the filtered position/angle time series.
